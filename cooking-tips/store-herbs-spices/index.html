The Right Way to Store Common Herbs and Spices

Keep aromatics lively by managing light, heat, moisture, and time.

By Daniel Hart•June 14, 2026

A spice cabinet can look permanent, but its contents are always changing. Dried herbs and ground spices do not suddenly become unsafe on the date printed on a jar; they gradually lose the aromatic oils that make them useful. Good storage slows that fading and makes it easier to notice when a jar has become little more than colored dust.

The four enemies: heat, light, air, moisture

The shelf above a stove is convenient and usually the worst place for spices. Heat rises, steam enters opened jars, and bright light dulls color. Choose a cool drawer or cupboard a few steps from the cooking area. Clear jars are fine inside a dark cabinet; on an open shelf, opaque tins offer better protection.

Close lids promptly. Every open minute releases aroma and exposes the contents to humid kitchen air. Never shake a jar directly over a steaming pot. Spoon out what you need, or shake it into your palm first, so condensation does not collect inside.

Whole spices last longer

Seeds, bark, and whole pods protect their aromatic compounds better than ground versions. Cumin seed, coriander, peppercorns, and nutmeg may remain vivid for several years if stored well. Ground spices have far more surface exposed to air and are often noticeably weaker after one or two years.

This does not mean everyone needs a dedicated grinder. Buy whole versions of the spices you use often or those whose aroma matters most. Toast them briefly in a dry pan, cool, and grind only what the dish requires. Pre-ground turmeric or cinnamon can still be practical when convenience means you will actually cook with it.

Dried herbs need a gentler test

Leafy herbs such as basil, parsley, and dill fade faster than sturdy rosemary, thyme, and oregano. Rub a pinch between your fingers and smell it. If the aroma is faint, the herb will add little to dinner. Color is another clue: olive or brown leaves have usually passed their flavorful peak.

Label what the package forgot

Write the purchase month and year on the lid. This is more useful than trying to remember whether a jar came from last winter or three winters ago. When buying from bulk bins, transfer spices to clean, fully dry containers and label both the name and date. Similar-looking powders are surprisingly difficult to identify later.

Do spices belong in the refrigerator?

Usually no. Repeatedly moving a jar between cold storage and a warm, humid kitchen can create condensation. The freezer can help with large quantities of expensive whole spices, but divide them into small airtight portions so the main supply is not opened repeatedly. Refrigerate specialty pastes and fresh spice mixtures according to their labels.

Buy for the kitchen you have

Large jars seem economical until half their aroma disappears unused. Purchase small amounts, especially of ground spices used only for one holiday dish. A focused collection of fifteen lively seasonings is more valuable than sixty tired ones.

When an old spice fails the smell test, replacement is usually the answer. Adding three times as much can make a dish dusty or bitter without restoring the missing fragrance. Empty the jar, wash and dry it completely, and give the new spice a date. The cabinet will remain smaller, clearer, and far more expressive.

Organize by use, not appearance

Alphabetical rows look orderly until cumin and coriander disappear behind rarely used cake decorations. Keep everyday seasonings within easy reach and group the rest by the way you cook: baking spices together, whole spices together, chile powders together. Put blends where their ingredients can be checked before extra salt is added.

A shallow drawer with labels on the lids often works better than a deep cabinet. Whatever the system, avoid stacking jars three rows deep. Visibility is part of freshness; a spice you cannot see is likely to be bought twice and used half as often.
