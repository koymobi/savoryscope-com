How to Keep Vegetables Fresh for Longer

Smart storage habits that protect texture, flavor, and your grocery budget.

By Emma Collins•July 23, 2026

Vegetables rarely spoil because the refrigerator is simply “too cold.” More often, they lose quality because they are stored with the wrong amount of moisture, packed too tightly, or forgotten in an opaque drawer. A few small habits can add days to delicate produce and weeks to sturdier items.

Start before the refrigerator

Choose vegetables that are firm and lively, without damp spots or deep bruises. Once home, remove tight rubber bands and twist ties. They damage stems and restrict air. Unless produce is visibly dirty, wait to wash it until you are ready to use it. Water trapped on leaves encourages decay.

Leafy greens need controlled moisture

Lettuce, spinach, and chard wilt when they dry out but turn slimy when sealed while wet. If you wash them in advance, spin them very dry. Wrap loosely in a clean towel or paper towel and place in a container or reusable bag with a little air. Replace the towel if it becomes soaked.

Whole heads last longer than cut leaves. If a head of lettuce droops, trim the base and soak the leaves in ice water for ten minutes. This cannot reverse spoilage, but it often restores crispness to merely dehydrated greens.

Keep roots and tops apart

Carrot, beet, and radish greens continue drawing moisture from their roots. Remove the tops before storage, leaving a short stem. Store the roots in a closed container in the crisper drawer. The greens are edible; use fresh beet tops like chard and turn carrot tops into a herby sauce.

Herbs are not all alike

Tender herbs such as parsley and cilantro do well with their stems in a jar of water, loosely covered in the refrigerator. Basil dislikes refrigerator temperatures and is often happier in water on the counter, away from direct sun. Woody herbs—rosemary, thyme, sage—can be wrapped in a barely damp towel and chilled.

Give mushrooms and peppers breathing room

Mushrooms release moisture and deteriorate quickly in sealed plastic. A paper bag or a container lined with a dry towel keeps them from becoming slick. Bell peppers prefer the crisper drawer and should remain whole until needed; cut edges soften fast.

Know what stays out

Potatoes and onions belong in cool, dark, ventilated places, but not together. Onions release gases and moisture that encourage potatoes to sprout. Winter squash also keeps well outside the refrigerator until cut. Tomatoes have better flavor and texture at room temperature; refrigerate only very ripe ones you need to hold, then let them warm before eating.

Use the drawer settings

A high-humidity crisper slows water loss and suits leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, and most vegetables that wilt. A low-humidity drawer lets ethylene gas escape and is useful for many fruits. Keep strong ethylene producers—apples, ripe bananas, pears, avocados—away from sensitive vegetables such as broccoli and leafy greens.

Finally, make the refrigerator visible. Put older vegetables at the front, keep a small “use first” container, and check it before shopping. Storage can preserve quality, but cooking what you bought is the habit that prevents the most waste.

Freezing is a form of rescue

When produce is unlikely to be eaten in time, move it to the freezer before quality collapses. Chop bell peppers and scallions and freeze them on a tray; they can go directly into cooked dishes. Blanch broccoli, green beans, and sturdy greens briefly, chill in ice water, dry, and freeze in portions. Their texture will change, but they remain useful in soup, pasta, and stir-fries.

Some vegetables are better cooked first. Roast a tray of soft tomatoes or eggplant, cool it quickly, and freeze the cooked pieces. A bunch of herbs can be blended with oil into a rough paste and frozen in small cubes. Freezing does not save a vegetable that has already spoiled, but it can interrupt the familiar slide from “I should use that” to the compost bin.
