Why Certain Foods Become Comfort Foods

Memory, repetition, and belonging help explain why some dishes feel like home.

By Daniel Hart•May 3, 2026

A comfort food does not have to be rich, beige, or cooked by a grandmother. It can be a sour soup, a packaged noodle, ripe mango with salt, or toast made exactly the way a parent once cut it. What makes the food comforting is not a universal flavor profile. It is the web of memory, body, habit, and belonging attached to the bite.

Memory arrives through the senses

Smell and taste are closely tied to brain regions involved in emotion and autobiographical memory. An aroma can bring back a kitchen before a person has consciously named the dish. Texture matters too: the slip of noodles, the crust of a particular bread, or the soft grain of porridge can feel familiar before it feels delicious.

These associations are learned through repetition. A soup served during childhood illness may later signal care. A snack eaten after school can recall safety and freedom. The food becomes a cue for the situation that surrounded it.

Comfort is often social

We sometimes picture comfort eating as solitary, but many comfort foods are tied to people. Dumplings folded at a table, a holiday casserole, or rice cooked by a partner carries evidence of shared labor. Even when eaten alone years later, the dish can create a temporary sense of company.

Migration makes this connection especially visible. Ingredients and recipes help people rebuild continuity in a new place. A familiar spice mixture or brand of tea can shrink distance. At the same time, substitutions made abroad may become treasured family traditions of their own.

The body likes predictability

Familiar foods reduce uncertainty. We know how they will taste, how filling they will be, and whether we can manage them when tired. Warm, soft foods are often easy to eat and digest. Carbohydrate-rich dishes can feel satisfying, while fat and salt intensify pleasure. Biology contributes, but it does not explain why one person wants macaroni and another wants congee.

Culture teaches the category

In the United States, surveys often produce pizza, ice cream, chicken soup, or macaroni and cheese. In Japan, rice dishes or miso soup may carry similar weight. Across the Caribbean, Latin America, Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East, comfort may appear as particular stews, breads, porridges, or rice dishes. These broad examples still miss the household level, where the true category lives.

Commercial foods can become authentic comfort foods too. Instant noodles eaten in a college dorm or a biscuit shared on long train trips can hold real memory. Nostalgia does not check whether something was made from scratch.

Why the recipe never tastes exactly right

People often recreate a remembered dish and find it technically correct but emotionally incomplete. Ingredients may have changed, yet context is usually the missing seasoning. The original meal included a room, a season, voices, and the fact that someone else cooked it. No written recipe can reproduce all of that.

Comfort without judgment

Food can soothe, and using it for comfort is not a moral failure. It becomes worth examining only when it is the sole response to distress or leaves someone feeling worse. Sometimes the needed comfort is the meal; sometimes it is rest, company, or support alongside the meal.

The most enduring comfort foods hold two times at once. They are eaten in the present, with today’s appetite, while opening a small door to somewhere familiar. That is why a plain bowl prepared the right way can matter more than a spectacular restaurant plate. It does not merely taste good. It tells the eater, briefly and convincingly, that they know where they are.

New comforts can be made

Nostalgia is powerful, but comfort is not limited to childhood. A dish cooked during the first winter in a new apartment or shared weekly with a friend can acquire meaning through repetition. Traditions begin quietly. Today’s improvised soup may become the meal someone asks for years from now.
