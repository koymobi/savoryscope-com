How Coffee Culture Differs From Country to Country

From quick counter rituals to long conversations, coffee carries local rhythm.

By Emma Collins•July 17, 2026

Coffee may be a global commodity, but the act of drinking it remains intensely local. A cup can be swallowed standing at a counter, stretched across an afternoon, brewed for guests in a small pot, or carried onto a train. The bean travels; the ritual tells you where it landed.

Italy: the counter sets the pace

Italian espresso culture is quick and public. Many customers order at the bar, drink in a few minutes, and continue with the day. Espresso is simply caffè; milk drinks such as cappuccino are strongly associated with morning, though visitors are free to order as they like. The small cup is not about chasing maximum caffeine—it is a concentrated pause.

Türkiye: coffee as hospitality and memory

Turkish coffee is prepared finely ground in a small pot, often with sugar decided before brewing. It is poured unfiltered, allowing grounds to settle in the cup. The slow drinking suits conversation, and reading the patterns left by grounds remains a playful social tradition. Coffee here is not merely a beverage order but an offer of welcome.

Ethiopia: ceremony around coffee’s birthplace

Ethiopia contains diverse coffee practices, and the well-known coffee ceremony is an extended act of hospitality. Beans may be roasted in view of guests, ground, and brewed in a jebena. Multiple rounds are served. Fragrance, time, and company matter as much as the liquid itself.

Sweden: making room for fika

Fika is often translated as a coffee break, but the phrase misses its social intention. It means stopping with others—or deliberately alone—often with a pastry or sandwich. Workplaces may build fika into the day. The point is not elaborate brewing; it is permission to pause.

Vietnam: strength, sweetness, and patience

Vietnamese coffee is commonly associated with dark-roasted robusta brewed through a small metal phin. The slow drip may fall over sweetened condensed milk and ice. Cà phê trứng, egg coffee, turns whipped egg yolk and condensed milk into a rich topping. These drinks reflect local production, climate, and adaptation to historical shortages of fresh milk.

Australia and New Zealand: the café as craft

Both countries developed influential espresso-focused café cultures. The flat white—espresso with finely textured milk—has become international, and arguments over its exact origin continue across the Tasman Sea. Independent cafés, careful milk texture, and brunch culture are central to the experience.

United States: refills, specialty, and the road

American coffee culture holds several eras at once: diner drip coffee with refills, commuter cups, home machines, and a specialty movement focused on origin and brewing precision. The large takeaway cup reflects distance and work patterns as much as taste. A meticulous pour-over and a gas-station coffee are both recognizably part of the landscape.

Traditions overlap

These sketches are not national rules. An Italian may linger over filter coffee; a Swede may rush an espresso; a Vietnamese café may serve experimental light roasts. Migration, chains, social media, and specialty coffee have made brewing methods travel faster than ever.

What remains distinctive is the social agreement around the cup: whether to sit, when to drink milk, who pours, how long to stay, and what food belongs nearby. Learning those rhythms can be more revealing than ranking which country makes the “best” coffee. Taste matters, but so does the shape of the time created around it.

The cup is changing with climate and economics

Behind every ritual is a farming system under pressure. Rising temperatures, plant disease, volatile prices, and the cost of labor affect what farmers can grow and what drinkers pay. Specialty cafés increasingly discuss producer names, processing methods, and traceability, though that language does not automatically guarantee fair outcomes.

The future of coffee culture will be shaped not only by new brewing gadgets but by whether cultivation remains viable. Paying attention to origin can deepen appreciation, provided the farmer is treated as more than a romantic detail on a bag.
