How to Make Crispy Roasted Potatoes Every Time

The simple choices that create deeply golden edges and fluffy centers.

By Daniel Hart•June 18, 2026

Roasted potatoes sound straightforward, yet the difference between merely cooked and genuinely crisp is surprisingly wide. The best ones have a brittle, deeply browned shell and a soft interior that almost collapses under a fork. That contrast comes from managing moisture and surface texture, not from pouring on extra oil.

Choose a potato with enough starch

Russets produce the fluffiest centers and roughest crusts. Yukon Golds are creamier and hold their shape better. Either works; small waxy potatoes can brown nicely, but they rarely develop the same craggy coating. Cut the pieces to roughly the same size—about two inches is generous enough to protect the middle while the outside roasts.

Parboiling is worth the pot

Start the potatoes in well-salted water and bring them to a steady simmer. Cook until a knife meets only slight resistance at the center, usually eight to ten minutes after the water boils. They should be tender at the edges without falling apart.

Drain them thoroughly, return them to the warm pot, and leave the lid off for a minute. Steam is the enemy here. When the surfaces look dry, cover the pot and shake it firmly. This scuffs the softened edges into a starchy paste that will turn into the crispest part of the potato.

Heat the tray, not just the oven

Place a sturdy sheet pan in a 450°F (230°C) oven while it preheats. Carefully add two or three tablespoons of neutral oil to the hot tray, followed by the potatoes. They should sizzle on contact. Spread them out so every piece has breathing room; a crowded tray traps steam and gives you pale sides.

Roast for about twenty minutes before turning. Potatoes release from the pan more easily after a crust has formed, so do not fuss with them early. Flip with a thin metal spatula and continue roasting for another twenty to thirty minutes, until several sides are dark gold.

Season in stages

Salt the cooking water, then add a modest amount of salt before roasting. Delicate aromatics belong near the end. Garlic added too soon can burn, and fresh rosemary may become bitter. Toss either with the potatoes during the last ten minutes, or warm minced garlic and herbs briefly in oil and spoon that fragrant mixture over the finished tray.

Small details that matter

  • Do not line the pan with a silicone mat; direct contact with metal browns better.
  • A teaspoon of baking soda in the boiling water encourages rougher edges, especially with russets.
  • Serve immediately. The crust softens as potatoes sit in their own steam.

If a batch refuses to crisp, the usual causes are excess water, a cool pan, or too many potatoes in too little space. Fix those three things before adding more oil. Crispness is mostly about giving moisture somewhere to go.

What to do with leftovers

Refrigerated roasted potatoes lose their brittle shell, but they are far from finished. Reheat them in a dry skillet with a thin film of oil, pressing the cut sides against the pan. They become excellent breakfast potatoes, especially with onions and a fried egg. Chopped leftovers also make a sturdy base for a frittata or a warm salad with mustard dressing.

Avoid the microwave when crispness matters; it warms the center while making the surface leathery. An air fryer or hot oven is better. Spread the potatoes in a single layer and heat until the edges sizzle again. They will not be quite as ethereal as the first serving, but a squeeze of lemon and a pinch of flaky salt make the revival convincing.

One final note: salt the potatoes while their surfaces are still shimmering with hot oil. Fine salt adheres more evenly than large flakes; save flaky salt for a small finishing pinch. That timing keeps seasoning on the crust instead of at the bottom of the serving bowl.
