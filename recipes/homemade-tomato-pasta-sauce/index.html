A Simple Homemade Tomato Pasta Sauce for Busy Nights

A bright, dependable sauce built from pantry staples.

By Emma Collins•July 11, 2026

There are tomato sauces that simmer all Sunday and tomato sauces that understand it is Tuesday. This is the second kind: bright, savory, and ready in roughly the time it takes to boil pasta. It uses canned whole tomatoes because they are dependable year-round and usually less processed than pre-crushed versions.

Prep time5 minutes
Cook time25 minutes
Serves4

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, thinly sliced
  • 1/4 teaspoon red pepper flakes, optional
  • 1 can (28 ounces) whole peeled tomatoes
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt, plus more for pasta water
  • 1 small sprig basil or a few parsley stems
  • 12 ounces spaghetti, rigatoni, or another dried pasta
  • Finely grated Parmesan, for serving

Make the sauce

Warm the olive oil in a wide skillet over medium-low heat. Add the sliced garlic and cook until it smells sweet and the edges are barely gold, about one minute. Dark brown garlic tastes harsh, so pull the pan from the heat for a moment if it colors too quickly. Add the pepper flakes.

Pour the tomatoes into a bowl and crush them with your hands, removing any tough stem ends. Add the tomatoes and their juices to the skillet. Season with salt and tuck in the herb sprig. Bring the sauce to a lively simmer, then lower the heat enough to prevent splattering. Cook uncovered for twenty minutes, stirring now and then and breaking up larger pieces with a wooden spoon.

Bring pasta and sauce together

Meanwhile, boil the pasta in generously salted water. Stop cooking it about two minutes before the package suggests. Scoop out a cup of the cloudy pasta water, then drain.

Remove the herb sprig from the sauce. Add the pasta directly to the skillet and toss over medium heat. Splash in pasta water a little at a time until the sauce looks glossy and clings to each piece. This final minute in the pan is what makes the dish taste integrated rather than like plain noodles wearing tomato sauce.

Useful variations

For a rounder sauce, melt in a tablespoon of butter at the end. For puttanesca energy without a long ingredient list, add chopped olives and capers. A spoonful of ricotta on each bowl softens the acidity. If children object to tomato pieces, blend the sauce before the pasta goes in—but let it cool slightly and vent the blender lid.

Leftover sauce keeps for four days in the refrigerator and freezes well for three months. Freeze it without pasta, which becomes soft when reheated. A flat freezer bag thaws faster than a deep container and can be broken in half when you only need a small amount.

Why canned tomatoes vary

Some cans are naturally sweet and dense; others are thin or sharply acidic. Taste after the sauce has simmered for ten minutes. If it is harsh, first add salt—under-seasoned tomato often reads as sour. If the seasoning is right but the edge remains, a small knob of butter can soften it. Sugar should be a last, tiny adjustment rather than an automatic ingredient.

The width of the pan matters too. A broad skillet reduces watery tomatoes quickly and gives pasta room to toss. In a narrow saucepan, allow another ten minutes. Look for the moment when a spoon dragged across the bottom leaves a trail that closes slowly. The sauce should be loose enough to move, since pasta and cheese will thicken it further.

Making it for different pasta shapes

Long pasta prefers a sauce loose enough to coat strands without gathering in clumps. Short tubes can handle a slightly thicker reduction because tomato settles into their openings. If the sauce becomes too dense while waiting, do not add more oil. Loosen it with reserved pasta water, one spoonful at a time, over low heat.

For baked pasta, stop reducing earlier than you would for spaghetti. The oven removes more moisture, and a sauce that looks perfect in the skillet may leave the casserole dry. Keep it visibly fluid before assembly.
