Lentils, Sorted: Which Type Works Best for What

Brown, green, red, and black lentils may share a shelf, but they do not share a texture—or the same best use.

By Maya Reed•September 17, 2026

Lentils are often sold as if color were cosmetic. It is not. Red lentils collapse into a soft puree while black lentils can hold a glossy, bead-like shape; brown lentils make an easy soup while sturdy green ones stay distinct in a vinaigrette. Buying the wrong bag is not a disaster, but it can turn a planned salad into something closer to dal.

This field guide focuses on the four supermarket families most home cooks encounter. Names overlap between regions and brands, so use the physical cues—size, whether the lentil is split, and how it behaves in simmering water—alongside the label.

First, know what all lentils have in common

Lentils are pulses: the dry edible seeds of legume plants. Unlike most dried beans, they do not need an overnight soak. Their small size lets water reach the center during a normal simmer. Rinse them in a fine-mesh sieve, spread them briefly on a plate, and remove any tiny stones or field debris before cooking.

Use a generous pot even for a small amount. Lentils foam as they begin to boil and need space to move. Start with roughly three cups of water or stock for every cup of whole lentils; split red lentils destined for a thick puree can use less. Bring the pot just to a boil, then lower it to a steady simmer. A violent boil knocks skins loose and breaks even varieties that are known for holding their shape.

Cooking times on packages are estimates. Age, variety, water chemistry, and desired texture all matter. Begin tasting five minutes early. The only reliable definition of “done” is the texture your dish needs.

Brown lentils: the weeknight generalist

Brown lentils are usually inexpensive and forgiving. Cooked gently, they remain recognizable but soften enough to thicken the liquid around them. That combination is ideal for vegetable soup, lentil loaf, taco filling, and the base beneath mashed potatoes in a meatless shepherd's pie.

Their flavor is earthy but not aggressive, so they take well to onion, carrot, celery, tomato, cumin, thyme, mustard, and smoked paprika. If a recipe simply says “lentils,” brown is often the safest guess unless the finished dish is supposed to be creamy or salad-like.

Stop cooking when the centers are tender but the skins have not burst widely. Drain them for a side dish; leave them in their cooking liquid for soup. If they go five minutes too far, lean into it: mash a portion against the pot to make the soup intentionally thick.

Green lentils: built for a fork

Large green lentils and smaller French-style green lentils are not always interchangeable, but both tend to hold their shape better than common brown lentils. The French type, often called lentilles du Puy when it meets the protected regional designation, is small, mottled, and peppery. Other French-style lentils can cook similarly without carrying that name.

Use green lentils when the grains need to remain separate: a warm salad with roasted carrots, a side dish under salmon, or a bowl with bitter greens and goat cheese. Salt the cooking water, add a bay leaf or halved onion, and dress the lentils while they are warm. Their surface accepts vinaigrette more readily before they cool completely.

For salad, test by pressing one between your tongue and palate. It should yield without dissolving. Drain immediately and spread on a tray for a few minutes so carryover heat does not push them into softness.

Red lentils: designed to disappear

Most red lentils sold in supermarkets are split and hulled. Removing the skin and splitting the seed exposes the interior, so they cook quickly and lose their shape. That is a strength. They create body without a blender and carry spices evenly through dal, Turkish-style lentil soup, and tomato-based stews.

Rinse until the water becomes less cloudy, but do not chase perfectly clear water. Simmer with turmeric, cumin, ginger, or garlic, and stir more often as the mixture thickens. Red lentils settle and can scorch on the bottom of a narrow pot. Add hot water when the puree becomes thicker than the finished dish should be; it will continue tightening as it cools.

They are not a convincing substitute in a composed salad. If that is the only bag available, change the plan rather than fighting the ingredient. Make a smooth soup, a spiced dip, or a soft bed for roasted vegetables.

Black lentils: small, polished, and sturdy

Black lentils are often sold as beluga lentils because their cooked appearance resembles caviar. They are small, dark, and relatively firm, with a flavor that can read mineral or chestnut-like. Their color makes pale cheese, orange squash, herbs, and yogurt look especially vivid on the plate.

Simmer them gently and keep the cooking liquid below a rolling boil. A splash of oil in the drained lentils helps preserve their sheen. They make an excellent room-temperature side with lemon and herbs, and they can be folded into rice or grains for contrast. Because they cost more than brown lentils, use them where their appearance and texture are visible rather than hiding them in a pureed soup.

Salt, acid, and the stubborn-lentil myth

Salt does not automatically keep lentils hard. Seasoning the cooking water helps flavor reach the interior, especially for whole green and black lentils. Acid is more complicated. Tomatoes, wine, and generous vinegar can slow softening, particularly when added early. If predictable tenderness matters, cook lentils mostly tender before adding strongly acidic ingredients.

Old lentils are a more common cause of stubbornness. A bag that has spent years in a warm cupboard may cook unevenly and never become as creamy as fresh stock. There is no date test that reveals age perfectly, so buy from a store with good turnover and label pantry containers when you bring them home.

For distinct lentils

Use a wide pot, gentle simmer, and enough water. Taste early, drain promptly, and season while warm.

For creamy lentils

Choose split red or yellow lentils, use slightly less liquid, stir as they soften, and add more hot water only when needed.

A base method for whole lentils

  1. Rinse and inspect

    Place one cup of lentils in a sieve, rinse under cool water, and pick out debris.

  2. Cover generously

    Add lentils to a pot with three cups of water, half an onion, a bay leaf, and a pinch of salt.

  3. Simmer quietly

    Bring to a boil, immediately lower the heat, and keep only small bubbles moving. Begin testing at twenty minutes.

  4. Drain for the intended texture

    For salad, drain as soon as tender. For soup, keep the cooking liquid and continue with the remaining ingredients.

  5. Season with contrast

    Add olive oil, acid, herbs, and final salt while warm. Taste again after ten minutes because lentils absorb seasoning as they sit.

Batch cooking without five identical lunches

Cook whole lentils simply, then divide them. Toss one portion with mustard vinaigrette for a salad. Simmer another with tomato and smoked paprika for soup. Fold a third into sauteed mushrooms for toast or a grain bowl. Keeping the base neutral creates options without asking you to cook from zero.

Refrigerate cooked lentils in a covered container with a little cooking liquid so they do not dry out. For freezing, portion them flat and label the type and quantity. Softer brown lentils freeze well for soups; firm green and black lentils may lose a little definition but remain useful in warm dishes.

When texture goes off course

  • Still hard after a long simmer: add fresh hot water and keep cooking; the lentils may be old. Avoid adding acid until tender.
  • Skins split before centers soften: the boil was too aggressive. Lower the heat.
  • Watery red lentils: simmer uncovered and stir. They thicken substantially during a short rest.
  • Mushy salad lentils: turn them into soup, patties, or a spread instead of trying to restore lost structure.

Questions from the pantry

Do lentils need soaking?

No. Soaking can shorten cooking slightly, but it removes the main convenience lentils offer. Rinse, inspect, and simmer.

Are canned lentils useful?

Yes, especially for salads and quick lunches. Rinse them gently and expect a softer texture than carefully simmered green or black lentils.

Can I swap lentils for beans?

Sometimes, but the cooking method and texture change. Lentils cook faster and are smaller. In soup the swap is easy; in a bean salad or chili, the result will feel fundamentally different.
